Event 1

SUMMARY REPORT: CLOUD ARCHITECT GAME SHOW

Event Objectives

  • Objective: Create a competitive but learning-focused activity where internship teams review cloud knowledge, AWS services and architecture design concepts through a head-to-head quiz format. The event encouraged participants to apply both foundational AWS knowledge and solution architecture thinking under time pressure.

Event Information

  • Event Name: Cloud Architect
  • Format: Team-based AWS architecture quiz competition
  • Participants: 8 teams, 5 members per team
  • Role: Attendee
  • Host/MC: AWS team
  • Main Topics: Cloud fundamentals, AWS core services, advanced AWS services, solution architecture design, AWS certification-style questions and architecture trade-off scenarios

Speakers and Facilitators

  • AWS Team: Host and MC for the competition. The AWS team introduced the rules, managed the match flow, announced scoring, controlled the use of special skills and kept the event moving between rounds.

Because this was a competition event rather than a seminar, the focus was not on speaker presentations. The main learning came from observing how each team reasoned through cloud architecture questions, discussed AWS service choices and handled pressure during direct matches.

Event Format

The event was organized as a knockout-style game show with 8 teams. Teams competed in direct 1-vs-1 matches. The team with the higher score advanced to the next round.

Round Structure

  • Quarterfinal round: 8 teams were divided into 4 pairs. Each match had 10 questions.
  • Semifinal round: 4 winning teams continued into the semifinal. The question difficulty increased compared with the first round.
  • Final round: 2 finalist teams were expected to compete for the championship, but the final match was postponed because the event time was limited.

If two teams had the same score after the official question set, the match moved to question 11. In the tie-break situation, the faster correct answer determined the winning team.

Competition Rules

Each team answered questions from a predefined question set. The questions were arranged from easier concepts to more difficult architecture scenarios. Later rounds placed more emphasis on advanced AWS services, system design trade-offs and AWS certification-style reasoning.

Scoring Logic

  • Correct answers added points to the team’s score.
  • Wrong answers could reduce the team’s score depending on the question and skill usage.
  • At the end of a match, the team with the higher score advanced.
  • If the score was tied, question 11 was used as a speed-based tie-breaker.

Special Skills

Each team had two special skills and could use each skill once:

  • Minimum Risk: Used when a team was unsure about an answer. If the answer was wrong, the team would not lose points. If the answer was correct, the team received only half of the question’s points.
  • Hope Star: Used when a team was confident in an answer. If the answer was correct, the team received double points. If the answer was wrong, the team lost double points.

These two skills made the competition more strategic. Teams had to decide not only which answer was correct, but also when to protect their score and when to take a higher-risk opportunity.

Key Highlights

AWS Knowledge Review Through Competition

The event reviewed AWS knowledge in a more active format than a normal lecture. Instead of only listening to explanations, participants had to follow each question, quickly recall related AWS concepts and compare possible answers.

The question topics covered:

  • compute services such as Amazon EC2, Auto Scaling, Elastic Load Balancing and container services,
  • storage services such as Amazon S3 and backup-related concepts,
  • networking topics such as VPC, subnet design, routing, security groups and high availability across Availability Zones,
  • identity and access topics such as IAM user, role, policy and least privilege,
  • monitoring and cost-related services such as Amazon CloudWatch and AWS Budgets,
  • architecture design questions similar to AWS Solution Architect scenarios.

Architecture Thinking Under Time Pressure

Many questions were not simple memorization questions. Some required teams to identify the best AWS service or architecture pattern under constraints such as cost, availability, scalability, security or operational simplicity.

This made the competition close to real solution architecture thinking. A technically correct service is not always the best answer if the scenario asks for a cheaper, more resilient or easier-to-operate design.

Strategy and Team Discussion

The two special skills created a clear difference between random guessing and calculated decision-making. A team could protect itself with Minimum Risk when the question was uncertain, or use Hope Star when the members were confident and wanted to create a score gap.

From the attendee perspective, this was a useful lesson in team communication. Strong teams did not only know AWS concepts; they also communicated quickly, filtered noisy opinions and chose the moment to use their limited skills.

Impressive Team: Ngu Dai Hiep

One team that left a strong impression was Ngu Dai Hiep. The team advanced through the earlier rounds and reached the final. Their performance stood out because they answered with confidence, handled pressure well and showed good teamwork during difficult architecture questions.

Although the final match was postponed due to limited time, Ngu Dai Hiep’s progress to the final made the event more memorable. It also showed that student teams can perform well when they combine AWS fundamentals, certification-style practice and fast group coordination.

Main Learning Points

Cloud Fundamentals Still Matter

The event reinforced that basic AWS topics are still the foundation for advanced architecture. IAM, VPC, EC2, S3, monitoring and cost control appeared repeatedly in different forms. Without these basics, it is difficult to answer more advanced solution design questions.

Architecture Questions Require Trade-off Thinking

For solution architect-style questions, the answer often depends on the keyword in the scenario. A question may focus on high availability, lowest cost, operational effort, security isolation or recovery time. Reading the requirement carefully is as important as remembering service names.

Teamwork Changes the Result

In a team competition, the strongest result did not come from one person answering everything. It came from dividing attention, listening quickly and making decisions before time ran out. This is similar to real cloud project work, where architecture choices are usually discussed by multiple people.

Certification Practice Helps Real Discussions

The question style was close to AWS certification thinking. Practicing certification questions can train the ability to eliminate wrong options, recognize AWS keywords and understand when one service should be preferred over another.

Event Experience

  • Practical Experience: The event created a lively learning environment. Watching 8 teams compete made the AWS knowledge review more engaging than a normal classroom session. The knockout format also made each round feel important because one wrong decision could affect the final result.
  • Technical Experience: The questions helped connect individual AWS services to larger architecture thinking. I could see how topics such as IAM, VPC, compute, storage, monitoring and cost optimization are often combined in real architecture scenarios.
  • Personal Impression: I was especially impressed by the team Ngu Dai Hiep because they reached the final and showed good teamwork. Their performance made the competition feel more serious and memorable, even though the final match had to be postponed.
  • Lesson Learned: For AWS learning, memorizing service names is not enough. It is important to understand the scenario, identify constraints and select the service or design pattern that best matches the requirement.

Applying to Work

  • Use AWS certification-style questions to review core services regularly.
  • When studying a service, connect it with architecture factors such as cost, security, scalability and availability.
  • Practice explaining why one AWS service is chosen over another, not just what the service does.
  • Improve team discussion skills because cloud architecture work often depends on fast, clear and evidence-based communication.
  • Build a personal checklist for architecture questions: requirement, constraint, service choice, trade-off and operational risk.
